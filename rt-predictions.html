Can a Transparent Model Explain a Prediction Market?

A prediction market prices where a movie’s Rotten Tomatoes score will settle on Monday. I forecast the same number at every minute of the week, from pedigree alone before any review exists to a posterior revised by each review that lands, and never let the model see the market.

The short answer is no, and that is the finding. Before the embargo lifts, the market holds private information my public features cannot represent, and it beats me decisively. Afterwards we converge, but so does simply averaging the reviews already posted, which turns out to be most of what the convergence is. Both are measured below.

A solo project: I collected the data, built the models, and keep the pipeline running unattended on Google Cloud.

The Question

A price exists for a number nobody has computed yet

A movie’s Tomatometer is the share of critics whose review is counted as positive. It first appears when the review embargo lifts, a few days before release, and it keeps moving as more critics file. There is a market where people trade contracts on where that percentage will sit at a fixed Monday cutoff, and that market carries a live price all week.

So a number is being priced before it is knowable. The price comes from somewhere I cannot see: industry tracking, test screenings, private conversations, gut feel. The question I wanted to answer is whether public information alone can land where that opaque price lands.

That question has two halves, and they are very different problems. Before the embargo lifts there is no review to read, so the only evidence is pedigree: who directed it, who wrote it, which studio released it, what it cost, how long it is. After the embargo lifts, reviews start landing one at a time, each timestamped, and the forecast has to absorb them as they arrive and keep projecting forward to the Monday deadline, when critics who have not yet filed will have filed. The first half is a cold-start prediction. The second is a sequential one, and it is the larger part of the work: the number you are predicting keeps moving while you predict it.

The thesis. If a transparent model built only from public information arrives at the market’s number, that convergence explains the price: it says the market is reading things anyone could read, not secrets. Where the two disagree, the disagreement itself is the finding. The goal was never a trading edge.

That framing dictated one hard rule, enforced everywhere in the code: the market price is never a feature and never a label. It appears only at evaluation time, as a yardstick I am scored against. A model that can see the price would learn to copy it and explain nothing.

What exactly is the benchmark

The venue is Kalshi, and it matters how their number is obtained, because the whole comparison rests on it. Kalshi does not trade the Tomatometer directly: each contract is a yes/no bet on a line, “will this film finish above 70?” But Kalshi also publishes its own implied score over time, a field called numerical_forecast, which is the curve their app shows traders. That published series is the benchmark, pulled at minute resolution over each contract’s life through a read-only, signed API. So the market’s number is theirs, not my reconstruction of theirs, which removes the obvious objection that I built the yardstick I am measured against. The separate line-by-line contracts are scored too, as calibrated probabilities, but they cover fewer films and are reported on their own denominator rather than blended in.

What Actually Moves

Scores drift down, and everyone already knows it

Because every review carries a second-level timestamp, the score can be reconstructed at any moment in history: the score at time T is the fresh rate among reviews posted by T. Doing that for a thousand films gives a thousand score curves, and they lean the same way. The critics who post first are self-selected enthusiasts, often from outlets courting early access. As the pool widens, harsher voices arrive and dilute the number.

Here is the trap. The average drift is common knowledge, so it is already in the price. Forecasting “it will fall about four points” adds nothing. The only thing worth anything is discrimination: separating the 88 that holds at 86 from the 88 that collapses to 68.

Illustrative, not measured (the measured comparison is further down). It shows two films that open at the same embargo number and settle 18 points apart. The dashed grey line is what everybody, including the market, already expects. The model earns its keep only if it can tell the green path from the orange one on Wednesday, which is why the drift stage is scored on rank correlation, not on average error.

That metric choice matters more than it sounds. Average error rewards a model for reproducing the priced-in average, which is why it hid a real signal for weeks. Scoring on discrimination instead revived a feature I had already retired: a measure of how harsh the critics who have already posted are, relative to their own history, lifts drift-ranking correlation from 0.34 to 0.54 at the earliest horizon.

The Data

None of this existed as a dataset

There is no download for any of this. Every asset below was collected, deduplicated and date-guarded by hand, with resumable idempotent collectors: network work on worker threads, all writes funneled through a single writer, every write an upsert, so an interrupted run costs minutes rather than a restart.

Two collection lessons were worth more than the rows they produced. The critic directory’s silent 100-per-letter cap is the kind of defect that never raises an error and quietly poisons every downstream statistic. And the market history looked capped at 17 usable settled contracts until I found the venue had reorganized its naming convention partway through; scanning properly recovered around 178. A number that looks small is sometimes a collection bug, not a fact, and in this case the difference decided whether the evaluation had any power at all.

The Model

A belief that starts from pedigree and is revised by every review

The forecast has to be valid at every moment, from before a single review exists to the minute before settlement, and it has to get sharper as evidence lands. That shape is a filter: hold a belief, then revise it as observations arrive. Four stages feed each other.

The chain as a filter. Stage 1 is the belief before evidence; stages 2 and 3 say how much evidence is still outstanding and which way it leans; stage 4 is the posterior at this minute. The market sits below the diagram on purpose: the dashed line is a comparison, not an input.

The strongest single ingredient in the cold start turned out to be the studio’s track record, shrunk toward the global mean so a studio with three films does not out-vote one with three hundred. Some coefficient signs read wrong until you think about who gets reviewed: English language is a mild negative, because the foreign films that reach American critics are already filtered down to the prestige ones, and a bigger budget is a negative too, because tentpoles review worse than mid-budget films. Video-game adaptations sit near 50, biopics near 75.

What Held Up, What I Killed

The honest pile

Most of what I built did not survive. That is the part of the project I would most want to be judged on, so it is on the page: each kill below rests on a measurement, and several of them saved weeks of collection I would otherwise have paid for.

The biggest negative result, stated first. Before the embargo lifts, in the cold-start phase where forecasting would be most valuable, the market beats this model decisively, and not by a little. I did not leave that as an impression; I measured the ceiling. The information in my public features explains 28% of the variance in the final score. The market explains 72%. The correlation between the market’s price and my own error is +0.59: it is right precisely where I am wrong. Adding my features on top of the market price moves its fit by about one point of R². The market holds pre-release information (tracking, screenings, buzz) that no public feature I found represents.

Three caveats I keep attached to that paragraph, because it is the one people quote. My model sits at about R² 0.20 against its own 0.28 feature ceiling, so a sliver of the gap is my modeling and not the data. And the result proves my current features cannot close it, not that no obtainable public feature could; I tested embargo lead, franchise and cast power since, and none of them closed it, which makes the private-information reading more likely without proving it. That analysis also predates the 140-market replay used in the chart below and has not been re-run against it, so treat the direction as solid and the second decimal as not.

That result is why the framing of this project is “explain,” not “beat.” Here is the whole comparison, on 140 settled Kalshi markets, with the one arm that makes it honest: a baseline that ignores the model entirely and just averages the reviews posted so far.

On the split protocol, because it is the first thing worth asking: these rows come from a per-vintage replay: to score a given film at a given minute, the model is refit on only the films that had already settled by that minute. The alternative, replaying history with today’s champion, leaks later settlements backwards into earlier forecasts, and the version of this table that does that is kept separately and is not what is plotted here.

Every row is replayed per vintage: the model scoring a given moment was fit only on films that had already settled at that moment, so no later settlement leaks backwards. All three arms are scored on the same films in every column, which is why n shrinks on the left: the baseline does not exist before the first review, so at 144 hours only 67 of the 140 markets qualify.

That chart is the most useful thing I learned, and it is not flattering. The model beats the do-nothing baseline by a clear margin only at the horizons where the market beats the model: 6.2 against the baseline’s 10.6 at six days, and 3.8 against 5.8 at four, paired gaps of 4.4 [1.9, 7.3] and 2.0 [0.8, 3.5] points. From 24 hours in, where the model most clearly beats the market, it is indistinguishable from simply averaging the reviews already posted. There is a mechanical reason: the served number is a blend of the model and the running average, weighted by the share of reviews still outstanding, so as that share goes to zero the forecast becomes the running average by construction. Reporting the late-horizon win over the market without this baseline would let an identity pose as a modeling result. So the baseline is scored as its own arm in every table, and the numbers I would actually defend are the far ones, against the baseline, not the near ones against the market.

Before the first review the running-average baseline does not exist, so that horizon needs a different floor: predicting the same number for every film. Across the settled markets the Tomatometer averages 67.4 with a standard deviation of 21.8, and always saying 67.4 earns a mean absolute error of 18.0 (17.5 if you say the median instead, and both are computed in-sample, which favors the constant). The model’s cold start is 11.2. The market is 8.2.

That is the shape of the cold-start result. Pedigree carries real information: knowing who directed it, who wrote it and who released it cuts the error against a constant by about a third. The market cuts it by about half. So this is a gap between two informative forecasters, not a case of useless features, which is what makes the private-information reading interesting rather than trivial.

✓ Held: the drift is real and directional

The score falls about 4.5 points on average, with falls outnumbering rises roughly three to one, and the effect survives controlling for opening-day sample noise.

But only the conditional mean is predictable. Most of the per-film spread is small-sample mean reversion, which is irreducible.

✓ Held: discrimination as the metric

Switching the drift stage from average error to rank correlation recovered a signal I had already retired: critic harshness lifts ranking correlation 0.34 → 0.54 at the earliest horizon.

Average error pays a model for reproducing the average everyone already prices. The metric was hiding the finding.

✓ Held: the arrival model

Predicting how many reviews are still to come cut error by about a third against the obvious baseline, and the live version predicts the increment rather than the total.

It is not a headline number, but it sets how far the score can still move, which every interval downstream depends on.

✗ Broke: the interval is honest on average, dishonest where it is narrowest

The served band is nominal 90%. On a 156-film holdout it covers 89% per film (95% interval 0.86 to 0.92, so consistent with nominal) at a median width of 16 points. Cut by horizon it falls apart exactly where the band is tightest: 70% coverage in the last 2 to 6 hours, 42% in the last 2, 28% at the cutoff.

This is the textbook failure of split conformal, measured rather than assumed: the method guarantees coverage on average, and a user lives in one cell, never in the average. In fairness to the band, at the cutoff it is 0.16 points wide and the median miss is 0.26 points, so it is a band collapsing to zero rather than a wild forecast. Still a misstatement of confidence, and still unfixed.

✗ Killed: the per-critic model

The plan was to predict each outstanding critic’s vote. It rests on harsh critics posting late, and the correlation between a critic’s harshness and when they typically post is −0.03. Outstanding critics are a lean-neutral draw.

The drift is real but it lives per event, not per person: on a given film, whoever reacts badly posts later. A fixed per-critic lean is mathematically blind to that. Killed on a structural reason rather than a disappointing score.

✗ Killed: star power, both ways

Cast fame correlates negatively with score (−0.24; A-listers anchor broad tentpoles). Cast quality correlates +0.25 and is real on its own, but adds +0.004 R² over fundamentals: it is collinear with director, writer and studio priors.

This null was computed from films already in hand, with coverage on 978 of 1,001 titles, and it vetoed a roughly 20,000-film collection run. A well-powered null is a budget decision.

✗ Killed: the contrarian-critic axis

I intended a second axis for critics who systematically disagree with consensus. Measuring first showed they barely exist: median agreement +0.50, with under 1% of critics below zero.

The feature pointed at an empty region of the data. Measuring before building cost an afternoon instead of a week.

✗ Demoted: embargo lead time

How early a studio lets critics publish looked predictive, but globally it is redundant: it is a symptom of budget, studio and director, all already encoded.

Except on films with no pedigree at all, debut directors and obscure studios, where it adds +0.118 R². Kept strictly as a fallback for the cold-start case it actually helps.

One more correction worth recording, because it was mine. An earlier version of my own write-up claimed the model “ties the market at every horizon.” It does not, and the sentence was sloppy in a specific way: it glued a clock-time claim to a review-count condition. Unconditioned, the far horizons are clear losses. Conditioned on at least one review existing, two horizons are statistically indistinguishable, but with 65 films and an interval three and a half points wide, “indistinguishable” cannot separate equal from the market is better, and the point estimate leans against me in both. An underpowered null is not a tie, and the comparison script now prints both slices side by side so the claim cannot be quoted loosely again.

One stage is designed but deliberately not built, and saying so is part of the record: the text of the reviews is unused. A model that reads the prose of the reviews already posted, to predict the verdicts of the critics still outstanding, is the obvious next accuracy engine. It is gated behind an experiment that has to show text beats verdicts alone before it earns the complexity.

In Production

It runs whether or not I am watching

A forecast that only exists in a notebook cannot be wrong in public, so the whole thing runs unattended on Google Cloud. Contracts are live for a few days at a time, which means onboarding a new film, polling the market, noticing new reviews, refreshing an explanation and recording what the forecast said at each minute all have to happen without me.

Two design constraints shape this picture. Exactly one process writes, so a minute-by-minute forecast log cannot be corrupted by two workers racing. And the pointer to the live model only moves when four gates pass, so a retrain that runs while nobody is looking cannot quietly make the product worse.

Why retraining needed gates rather than a cron line

The model gets better as contracts settle and become labeled data, so retraining weekly is obviously right. The risk is equally obvious: a job that trains and promotes on its own can promote something worse and nobody finds out until the forecasts look strange. Each gate answers a specific failure I have already had: the artifact loads but cannot serve; the new training set silently drops contracts the current champion learned from; the candidate looks better only because it is being scored on data it trained on; the candidate is fine on average but has moved its opening forecasts further in one week than any week in over two years of replayed history. The last one is the interesting gate, because it catches a model that is defensible on the test set and still behaving unlike itself.

Shipping without breaking the record

Deploys have to earn their way out. A push must have a green build for that exact commit before the deploy script will proceed; a dirty working tree or an unpushed commit stops it; images are built once and released by content hash so the thing tested is the thing that ships; the new revision takes no traffic until its heavy paths are warm and a contract check passes; and a failed post-deploy check rolls both services back on its own. That rollback has fired for real on a live deploy, which is the only way to know it works.

What it costs. The read API scales to zero, which keeps idle spend near nothing but means a first request after a quiet period waits roughly 35 to 40 seconds for a cold start. That is a real wait, and it is a deliberate trade: this is a portfolio system, and I would rather pay it than keep a warm instance running around the clock. The writing loop, which genuinely cannot miss a minute, runs on an always-on instance instead.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the project

The market never enters training

Not as a feature, not as a label, not as a target to distill from. It appears only in evaluation code.

Why: the entire question is whether public information can reach the market’s answer. A model that has seen the price would reproduce it and prove nothing. Giving up the single most predictive column available is the cost of being able to interpret the result at all.

Reconstruct history from timestamps instead of snapshots

Rather than recording the score daily going forward, the score at any past moment is recomputed from the reviews posted by then.

Why: waiting to accumulate snapshots would have meant a year before there was anything to model. Timestamps turn one collection pass into a thousand full historical curves, and make every horizon evaluable at once.

Measure before collecting

Two expensive collection runs were canceled by cheap proxy tests computed on data already in hand.

Why: a well-powered null on a proxy is evidence, and the alternative was days of collection to learn the same thing. The discipline is to ask what result would change the plan before paying for the data.

Date-guard every prior

A director’s track record for a target film is computed only from films released strictly before it, and a popularity field was dropped entirely because it reflects today, not release day.

Why: pedigree features are the easiest place in this project to leak the future. Guarding by date costs a WHERE clause; not guarding it invalidates every number on this page.

One writer, many readers

A single loop holds all write access to the forecast log; the API and every dashboard read.

Why: the deliverable is an append-only record of what was believed at each minute. Two writers racing produce a record that cannot be trusted retrospectively, and the whole evaluation rests on that record.

Intervals sized from residuals

Prediction bands are sized from held-out errors rather than from a distributional formula, and the coverage they actually delivered is measured by slice rather than quoted as a headline.

Why: a distributional assumption gives you a number that cannot be checked against anything. Sizing from real residuals at least makes the claim falsifiable, which is how the conditional-coverage failure above was found rather than assumed away. An interval that is confidently too narrow is the worst failure available, because it is the number a reader acts on.
