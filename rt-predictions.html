Can a Transparent Model Explain a Prediction Market?

Forecasting where a movie’s Rotten Tomatoes score will settle, against a market the model is never allowed to see.

The Question

People are betting on a number that does not exist yet

The short answer is no. Before the first reviews come out, the market beats my model badly. After the reviews start, we end up in the same place, but so does just averaging the reviews already published. Both of those are measured further down.

A movie’s Tomatometer score is simply the percentage of critics who liked it. The first score shows up a few days before release, when critics are allowed to publish, and it keeps changing as more of them post. On Kalshi, a betting site, people trade on where that percentage will land by a set deadline on Monday. There is a live price all week.

So people are putting money on a number nobody can look up yet. They know things I cannot see: how test screenings went, what the industry is hearing, plain gut feel. I wanted to know something simple. Using only public information, can I get to the same number they do?

That turns out to be two different problems.

Before any review exists, all I have is the film’s background: the director, the writer, the studio, the budget, the runtime. I have to guess the score from scratch.

Once reviews start appearing, they arrive one at a time, each with a timestamp. Now I have to keep updating my guess as each one lands, and still predict where it will finish on Monday, once the critics who have not posted yet have posted. This is the harder half, because the number keeps moving while you are trying to predict it.

Why bother. If a model anyone can inspect ends up at the same number as the market, that tells you the market is mostly reading public information, not secrets. If the two disagree, the disagreement is the interesting part. I was never trying to make money on it.

That gave me one rule I never broke: the model never sees the market price. Not while training, not as a hint, nothing. The price only shows up at the end, when I check my answer against it. A model that could see the price would just copy it, and copying proves nothing.

Where the market’s number comes from

This matters, because the whole comparison rests on it. Kalshi does not let you bet on the exact score. Each bet is yes or no on a line: will this film finish above 70?

But Kalshi also publishes its own running estimate of the final score, and that is the curve their app shows traders. That is the number I compare against, taken every minute of each contract’s life through a read-only connection. So the market’s number is their number, not my guess at what they think, which kills the obvious complaint that I made up my own scoreboard. I score the yes/no bets too, but fewer films have them, so I report those separately instead of mixing them in.

What Actually Moves

Scores drift down, and everyone already knows it

Every review comes with a timestamp, down to the second. That means I can rebuild what the score was at any past moment: count the reviews posted by then, and work out the percentage that were positive. Do that for a thousand films and you get a thousand score histories, and they nearly all lean the same way. The critics who post first tend to be the keen ones, often from outlets that get early access. As more critics pile in, the harsher ones drag the number down.

Here is the catch. Everyone knows scores fall, so that is already built into the price. Predicting “it will drop about four points” is worth nothing. The only thing worth anything is telling the films apart: spotting which 88 holds at 86, and which 88 falls to 68.

Illustrative, not measured (the measured comparison is further down). It shows two films that open at the same embargo number and settle 18 points apart. The dashed grey line is what everybody, including the market, already expects. The model earns its keep only if it can tell the green path from the orange one on Wednesday, which is why the drift stage is scored on rank correlation, not on average error.

How you score a model matters more than it sounds. If you grade on average error, a model gets rewarded for predicting the average fall that everyone already expects, which is why one real signal stayed hidden for weeks. Once I graded on which films fall furthest instead, a feature I had already thrown out came back. If the critics who have posted so far are harsher than they normally are, the film tends to keep falling. That lifted the ranking score from 0.34 to 0.54 at the earliest point, where 1.0 would be perfect.

The Data

None of this existed as a dataset

You cannot download any of this. I collected all of it myself, removed duplicates, and made sure nothing from the future leaked into the past. The collectors can be stopped and restarted without losing or double-counting anything, so an interrupted run costs a few minutes instead of starting over.

Two things I learned while collecting were worth more than the data itself. The critic directory quietly stops at 100 names per letter of the alphabet. It does not warn you; it just hands back a short list, and every statistic built on it would have been wrong. And I thought there were only 17 finished contracts to test against, until I found that Kalshi had changed how it named them partway through. Searching properly turned up about 178. A number that looks small is sometimes a bug, not a fact, and here it was the difference between having enough data to prove anything and not.

The Model

A belief that starts from pedigree and is revised by every review

The forecast has to work at any moment, from before a single review exists right up to the deadline, and it should get sharper as more reviews come in. So it works like a running estimate: start with a best guess, then update it every time something new arrives. Four pieces feed into each other.

The chain as a filter. Stage 1 is the belief before evidence; stages 2 and 3 say how much evidence is still outstanding and which way it leans; stage 4 is the posterior at this minute. The market sits below the diagram on purpose: the dashed line is a comparison, not an input.

The single most useful clue before any review exists is the studio’s track record. A studio with three films to its name gets less weight than one with three hundred, so a small sample cannot swing things.

A few results looked backwards until I thought about who gets reviewed at all. Films in English score slightly worse, because the foreign films that reach American critics are already the prestige ones. Bigger budgets also score worse, because blockbusters review worse than mid-budget films. Video game adaptations land near 50; biographical films near 75.

What Held Up, What I Killed

What worked, and what I threw away

Most of what I built did not work. I would rather be judged on that than on the parts that did, so it is all here. Nothing below was dropped on a hunch. Each one was measured first, and a few of those measurements saved me weeks of pointless data collection.

The worst result, up front. Before any reviews exist, which is exactly when a forecast would be most useful, the market beats my model badly. I did not just leave it at that, I measured how badly. Everything I can know publicly accounts for 28% of what decides the final score. The market accounts for 72%. And when I check where I go wrong, the market is most confident exactly where I am most wrong. Bolting my features onto the market price barely improves it. The market knows things before release, from screenings and industry chatter, that nothing public tells me.

Three things I always say alongside that, because it is the paragraph people quote.

  1. My model does not quite reach even the 28% its own data allows, so a small part of the gap is my modeling rather than the data.
  2. This shows my features cannot close the gap. It does not prove no public feature could. I have since tried three more and none of them closed it, which makes the private-information explanation likelier but not proven.
  3. This comparison is older than the 140-film test below and I have not re-run it, so trust the direction and not the exact figures.

That is why this project is about explaining the market, not beating it. Here is the full comparison across 140 finished Kalshi contracts, including the one thing that keeps it honest: a dumb baseline that ignores my model completely and just averages the reviews published so far.

One thing worth checking before you trust any of it: could the model be cheating by knowing the future? To score a film at a given minute, I retrain it using only films that had already finished by that minute. The lazy alternative is to replay history using today’s model, which has seen results that had not happened yet. I keep that version separately, and it is not what is plotted here.

That chart is the most useful thing I learned, and it is not flattering.

The model beats the do-nothing baseline by a clear margin only at the horizons where the market beats the model.

Six days out it is 6.2 against the baseline’s 10.6, and four days out 3.8 against 5.8: paired gaps of 4.4 [1.9, 7.3] and 2.0 [0.8, 3.5] points. But from 24 hours in, where the model most clearly beats the market, it is indistinguishable from simply averaging the reviews already posted.

There is a plain reason for that. My forecast is a mix of the model’s guess and the average of the reviews so far, and the fewer reviews are still to come, the more weight the average gets. By the deadline there is nothing left to come, so my forecast is the average. Claiming that as a win would be claiming credit for arithmetic. So the dumb baseline is scored in every table I produce, and the results I would actually stand behind are the early ones against the baseline, not the late ones against the market.

Before the first review the running-average baseline does not exist, so that horizon needs a different floor: predicting the same number for every film. Across the settled markets the Tomatometer averages 67.4, and always saying 67.4 is the floor to beat.

Pedigree alone cuts the constant’s error by about a third; the market cuts it by about half. The distance between the blue bar and the dark one is the private information the model cannot see. The constant is computed in-sample, which favors it, and saying the median instead of the mean scores 17.5.

So the cold start is a gap between two informative forecasters, not a case of useless features.

✓ Worked: the drift is real, and it has a direction

The score falls about 4.5 points on average, with falls outnumbering rises roughly three to one, and the effect survives controlling for opening-day sample noise.

But only the conditional mean is predictable. Most of the per-film spread is small-sample mean reversion, which is irreducible.

✓ Worked: grading on the right thing

I stopped grading on average error and started grading on whether the model ranks films correctly, worst faller to best holder. A signal I had already discarded came straight back: 0.34 to 0.54, where 1.0 would be perfect.

Average error pays you for predicting the average drop everyone already expects. The way I was measuring was hiding the result.

✓ Worked: predicting how many reviews are left

Guessing how many more reviews are still coming cut the error by about a third compared to the obvious approach.

Not exciting on its own, but it decides how far the score can still move, and every confidence range I publish depends on it.

✗ Broke: my “90% sure” range is only right on average

Every forecast comes with a range that is supposed to contain the true answer 90% of the time. Across 156 films it does: 89%. But if you look only at the final hours, it fails badly. In the last few hours it is right 70% of the time, in the last two hours 42%, and at the deadline itself 28%.

This is the known weakness of the method, and I measured it rather than assumed it away. It promises to be right 90% of the time overall, but nobody reads an overall average, they read one film at one moment. In its defence, by the deadline the range has narrowed to a fraction of a point and the typical miss is about a quarter of a point, so it is a range shrinking to nothing rather than a wild forecast. It still overstates confidence, and I have not fixed it.

✗ Killed: predicting each critic individually

The idea was to guess how each critic who has not posted yet will vote. It only works if harsh critics tend to post late. They do not: the link between how harsh a critic is and how late they usually post is essentially zero. The critics still to come are a coin flip.

Scores really do fall late, but it is not because harsh people are late in general. On any given film, whoever happens to dislike that film posts later. Knowing a critic’s usual temperament cannot capture that. I killed it because the idea was broken, not because the score was disappointing.

✗ Killed: star power, in both forms

Famous casts score slightly worse, because big stars headline big crowd-pleasers that critics tend to mark down. Casts with a good track record do score better on their own, but once I already know the director, writer and studio, the cast adds almost nothing new.

I worked this out from films I already had, covering 978 of 1,001 titles, before collecting anything. It talked me out of scraping roughly 20,000 more films. A convincing negative result is worth as much as a positive one here, because it saves the work.

✗ Killed: the contrarian critic

I wanted to track critics who reliably disagree with everyone else. I measured first, and they barely exist: fewer than 1% of critics lean against the consensus at all.

The idea was pointing at an empty part of the data. Checking first cost an afternoon; building it first would have cost a week.

✗ Demoted: how early critics are allowed to publish

Studios that are confident let critics publish early, so this looked promising. In general it is redundant: it mostly just reflects the budget, studio and director, which I already know.

With one exception. For films where I know nothing about the people involved, first-time directors and unknown studios, it genuinely helps, so I kept it only for that case.

One more correction worth recording, because it was mine. An earlier version of my own write-up claimed the model “ties the market at every horizon.” It does not, and the sentence was sloppy in a specific way: it glued a clock-time claim to a review-count condition.

Unconditioned, the far horizons are clear losses. Conditioned on at least one review existing, two horizons are statistically indistinguishable, but with 65 films and an interval three and a half points wide, “indistinguishable” cannot separate equal from the market is better, and the point estimate leans against me in both.

An underpowered null is not a tie.

The comparison script now prints both slices side by side, so the claim cannot be quoted loosely again.

One stage is designed but deliberately not built, and saying so is part of the record: the text of the reviews is unused. A model that reads the prose of the reviews already posted, to predict the verdicts of the critics still outstanding, is the obvious next accuracy engine. It is gated behind an experiment that has to show text beats verdicts alone before it earns the complexity.

In Production

It runs whether or not I am watching

A forecast sitting in a notebook on my laptop can never be publicly wrong, which makes it easy to fool yourself. So the whole thing runs on Google Cloud without me. Contracts only last a few days, so picking up new films, checking the market, spotting new reviews, and recording what the forecast said at every minute all have to happen on their own.

Two design constraints shape this picture. Exactly one process writes, so a minute-by-minute forecast log cannot be corrupted by two workers racing. And the pointer to the live model only moves when four gates pass, so a retrain that runs while nobody is looking cannot quietly make the product worse.

Why retraining needed gates rather than a cron line

Every time a contract finishes, I learn the real answer for one more film, so retraining every week is obviously worth doing. The risk is just as obvious: a job that retrains and swaps in the new model by itself can quietly swap in a worse one, and nobody notices until the forecasts start looking odd. Each check below exists because that failure has already happened to me in that exact way.

  1. The artifact loads but cannot actually serve.
  2. The new training set silently drops contracts the current champion learned from.
  3. The candidate looks better only because it is being scored on data it trained on.
  4. The candidate is fine on average but has moved its opening forecasts further in one week than any week in over two years of replayed history.

The last one is the interesting gate, because it catches a model that is defensible on the test set and still behaving unlike itself.

Shipping without breaking the record

Deploys have to earn their way out. Five things have to be true before a change reaches anyone:

  1. the commit has a green build, that exact commit, or the deploy script refuses to run;
  2. the working tree is clean and the commit is pushed;
  3. images are built once and released by content hash, so the thing tested is the thing that ships;
  4. the new revision takes no traffic until its heavy paths are warm and a contract check passes;
  5. a failed post-deploy check rolls both services back on its own.

That rollback has fired for real on a live deploy, which is the only way to know it works.

What it costs. The part that answers questions shuts itself off when nobody is using it, so it costs almost nothing to idle. The price is that the first request after a quiet spell waits about 35 to 40 seconds while it starts up. That is a real wait and I chose it: this is a portfolio project, not a business, so I would rather wait than pay to keep it running all night. The part that records forecasts cannot afford to miss a minute, so that one stays on permanently.

Key Decisions

The calls that shaped the project

The market never enters training

Not as a feature, not as a label, not as a target to distill from. It appears only in evaluation code.

Why: the entire question is whether public information can reach the market’s answer. A model that has seen the price would reproduce it and prove nothing. Giving up the single most predictive column available is the cost of being able to interpret the result at all.

Reconstruct history from timestamps instead of snapshots

Rather than recording the score daily going forward, the score at any past moment is recomputed from the reviews posted by then.

Why: waiting to accumulate snapshots would have meant a year before there was anything to model. Timestamps turn one collection pass into a thousand full historical curves, and make every horizon evaluable at once.

Measure before collecting

Two expensive collection runs were canceled by cheap proxy tests computed on data already in hand.

Why: a well-powered null on a proxy is evidence, and the alternative was days of collection to learn the same thing. The discipline is to ask what result would change the plan before paying for the data.

Date-guard every prior

A director’s track record for a target film is computed only from films released strictly before it, and a popularity field was dropped entirely because it reflects today, not release day.

Why: pedigree features are the easiest place in this project to leak the future. Guarding by date costs a WHERE clause; not guarding it invalidates every number on this page.

One writer, many readers

A single loop holds all write access to the forecast log; the API and every dashboard read.

Why: the deliverable is an append-only record of what was believed at each minute. Two writers racing produce a record that cannot be trusted retrospectively, and the whole evaluation rests on that record.

Intervals sized from residuals

Prediction bands are sized from held-out errors rather than from a distributional formula, and the coverage they actually delivered is measured by slice rather than quoted as a headline.

Why: a distributional assumption gives you a number that cannot be checked against anything. Sizing from real residuals at least makes the claim falsifiable, which is how the conditional-coverage failure above was found rather than assumed away. An interval that is confidently too narrow is the worst failure available, because it is the number a reader acts on.
